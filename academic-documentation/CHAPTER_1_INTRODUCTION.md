# CHAPTER ONE — INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background to the Study

Educational institutions depend on many administrative processes that appear routine but have direct effects on student welfare, accountability, and institutional trust. Student identification, permit issuance, payment confirmation, elections, announcements, document circulation, and operational reporting all contribute to how students interact with their university and with student governance structures. When these processes are handled manually or through disconnected tools, simple activities can become slow, difficult to audit, and vulnerable to error. For a student governance body such as the Students' Representative Council (SRC), the problem has both technical and governance dimensions. It affects the credibility of decisions, the reliability of student records, and the quality of service delivered to students.

Universities have gradually moved many administrative functions from paper-based records to digital systems. This movement has been influenced by the need to improve access to information, reduce duplication of records, support remote interaction, and make institutional processes easier to monitor. In a typical university environment, students expect to receive information quickly, complete requests without unnecessary physical movement, and verify their status using channels that fit their everyday use of mobile devices. Staff and executives also need reliable records that can support decisions, reduce disputes, and provide evidence of actions taken. These expectations have made digital governance systems more relevant to student administration and institutional operations.

Digital governance in an educational institution refers to the use of information systems to coordinate administrative, communication, identity, and decision-making processes. In the context of student governance, it may include student registration records, permit management, election voting, publication of announcements, event communication, document access, role-based administrative access, and reporting. The aim is not simply to replace paper with screens. A well-designed digital governance platform provides a structured way to manage records, enforce access rules, and preserve operational evidence. This becomes important when several officers or departments participate in the same workflow.

Manual student verification remains a common challenge in many institutions. Verification may be required during examinations, events, elections, permit checks, student service requests, or access to specific facilities. In a manual process, staff may depend on printed lists, physical permit cards, handwritten receipts, or visual inspection of student details. These methods can work in small settings, but they become less reliable as the number of students and transactions increases. A person checking a permit manually may have to search through paper records, compare names, inspect a card, or ask another office for confirmation. Each extra step adds delay and increases the chance of a wrong decision.

Student permit systems present a related problem. A permit is useful only when it can be issued reliably and verified when needed. If permit issuance is handled through manual forms and physical payment confirmation, the process may be slow for students and difficult to supervise for SRC executives. A student may pay for a permit but experience delays before receiving confirmation. Another student may present a permit that is expired, duplicated, altered, or assigned to someone else. Without a central permit record, security personnel and administrators may struggle to determine whether a permit is valid at the point of inspection. The weakness is not always caused by dishonesty. Sometimes the problem is that the verification process lacks a dependable source of truth.

Identity management is central to this issue. A student governance platform must be able to distinguish between a student record, a user account, a permit, a payment record, and a verification event. These items are related, but they are not the same. A student may exist in the institution's records before having an activated system account. A permit may be linked to a particular academic period. A payment may be pending, successful, failed, or manually reviewed. A verification event may confirm that a permit is active, or it may expose a mismatch between the presented identifier and the stored record. When these details are not connected in a structured system, identity verification becomes dependent on memory, physical documents, or informal communication.

Accountability is another major concern. In a manual environment, it is often difficult to determine who issued a permit, who verified a payment, who changed a student record, or who approved a candidate in an election. Paper forms and informal messaging channels can record some information, but they are not always consistent. They may also be misplaced, altered, or difficult to search. Auditability gives an institution the ability to reconstruct what happened during a workflow. For student governance, audit logging can support transparency by recording sensitive actions such as permit issuance, payment verification, NFC card registration, election voting, account activation, and administrative changes.

The need for accountability becomes clearer when financial and electoral activities are involved. Permit issuance may involve payments, and student elections may determine who represents the student body. These are sensitive processes. If payment verification is weak, permits may be issued without confirmed payment, or students who have paid may be delayed because their payment cannot be matched to a request. If election management is weak, there may be disputes about eligibility, duplicate voting, candidate approval, or result visibility. A student governance platform should therefore support both operational efficiency and procedural fairness.

Mobile systems have changed how students expect to interact with institutional services. Many students use mobile devices as their primary means of communication, information access, and online transaction. A student who can view permit status, submit a permit request, receive governance information, or participate in voting through a mobile application is less dependent on physical offices and notice boards. This does not remove the need for web-based administration, but it extends access to students in a form that is closer to their daily habits. For staff and executives, mobile access can also support verification and field operations where a desktop computer is not practical.

Near Field Communication (NFC) provides another opportunity for improving student verification. NFC is a short-range wireless communication technology that allows compatible devices to read identifiers from NFC cards or tags. In a student permit verification context, an NFC card can be associated with a student record and used as a quick verification token. A staff member or authorized user can scan the card with a compatible mobile device, allowing the system to check the linked student and permit status. This can reduce the time spent manually searching for student information, especially during high-volume verification activities such as examinations or organized SRC operations.

NFC verification, however, must be treated carefully. An NFC card should not be considered secure merely because it can be scanned. The system must still check whether the card is registered, active, linked to the correct student, and connected to a valid permit for the relevant academic period. It must also handle lost, replaced, or revoked cards. For privacy and security, raw card identifiers should not be exposed unnecessarily. The value of NFC in this study therefore lies in how it supports a controlled verification workflow rather than in the card alone. The card is only one part of a wider identity and permit management process.

Digital payment systems have also become relevant in institutional workflows. Students increasingly expect payment processes to be traceable and convenient. In a permit management context, digital payment integration can support self-service permit requests by allowing students to initiate payment, receive confirmation, and proceed through issuance without relying entirely on manual receipt checking. Payment verification remains important because a redirect or screenshot is not enough to prove that a transaction was successful. A reliable system should verify payment status through the payment provider and connect the result to the correct permit request. This helps reduce disputes and gives administrators clearer recovery options when a transaction fails or remains pending.

Student elections are another area where digitization can improve governance when implemented with proper controls. Elections require clear eligibility rules, candidate management, vote recording, and result handling. Manual election processes may work where the population is small and all participants are present, but they can become difficult to manage when students are dispersed or when voting records must be checked later. Electronic voting in a student governance platform can help enforce one vote per eligible student, maintain candidate approval workflows, and provide structured result data. It must also avoid casual treatment of voting as a simple poll. Election voting is more sensitive because it affects representation and must be designed with restrictions that preserve integrity.

Public communication is part of student governance as well. Students need reliable access to announcements, events, documents, executive profiles, election information, and permit request channels. When communication is spread across social media posts, messaging groups, printed notices, and informal announcements, students may receive inconsistent information. A public portal gives the SRC a more organized channel for publishing official content. It can also separate public information from administrative data so that students and visitors can access relevant content without exposing internal records.

Fragmented systems create several practical difficulties. A dashboard may exist for one activity, a spreadsheet for another, a payment receipt folder for another, and a messaging group for communication. These tools may solve immediate problems, but they do not form a coherent governance platform. A permit record may not be linked to a payment record. A student account may not be linked to an election eligibility check. A verification event may not be linked to the staff member who performed it. Over time, this fragmentation makes reporting difficult because data must be gathered from several places before meaningful decisions can be made.

The proposed study is situated within this operational context. Knutsford University, like many higher education institutions, requires student-facing processes that are reliable, accessible, and accountable. The SRC has governance responsibilities that involve students, executives, administrators, and security personnel. These responsibilities include communication, permit management, identity verification, elections, and reporting. A centralized student governance platform can help bring these workflows into a more coordinated system.

The project titled "NFC-Based Student Permit Verification and Governance Management System for Knutsford University" addresses this need by proposing a multi-platform student governance system. The system is designed to support permit management, NFC-based verification, self-service permit request, payment verification, election voting, public communication, audit logging, reporting, and mobile access. Its purpose is not to digitize one isolated task. Rather, it provides a structured platform where related student governance activities can share consistent records and controlled workflows.

The emphasis on a centralized platform is important. Permit issuance, student identity, payment confirmation, card registration, and verification logs are closely related. Treating them as separate manual activities weakens the ability to verify what has happened. A centralized system allows these workflows to refer to the same student and permit records. It also allows SRC executives and authorized personnel to view operational summaries instead of relying only on scattered documents or individual reports. This improves the practical management of student services and strengthens the evidence available for decisions.

In this study, the mobile application is considered an access channel for students and authorized staff, while the operations dashboard supports administrative work. The public portal supports official communication and self-service interaction. NFC supports faster verification at the point where a student card or identifier is presented. Payment verification supports traceable permit issuance. Audit logging and reporting support accountability. These parts are connected by the broader aim of improving student governance operations through a technically grounded information system.

The study therefore responds to a realistic institutional problem: student governance workflows often require more than isolated digital forms. They require identity consistency, payment traceability, verification speed, controlled access, communication channels, and records that can be reviewed. By combining these concerns into a single student governance platform, the proposed system seeks to improve the reliability of SRC operations while providing a foundation for future expansion.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Student governance operations can become inefficient when permit management, student verification, payment confirmation, election voting, public communication, and reporting are handled through manual or disconnected processes. In such an environment, students may be required to move between offices, present physical documents repeatedly, wait for manual confirmation, or depend on informal communication for updates. Administrators and SRC executives may also spend unnecessary time reconciling records from different sources before they can make decisions.

Permit fraud is one of the main concerns in a weak verification process. A permit may be altered, reused, duplicated, expired, or presented by a person other than the rightful holder. If verification depends mainly on visual inspection, the person performing the check may not have enough information to determine validity. Even where a printed permit contains a code or student details, the absence of a central verification process can make it difficult to confirm whether the permit is active, revoked, or linked to the correct academic period. This creates room for impersonation and weakens trust in the permit system.

Manual verification is also slow. During periods of high activity, such as examinations or SRC-organized events, several students may need to be checked within a short time. A manual process may require searching paper records, comparing names, inspecting receipts, or contacting another officer for confirmation. These steps consume time and can delay both students and staff. A slow verification process may also encourage shortcuts, especially when the number of students is high and the available personnel are limited.

Payment confirmation presents another operational difficulty. When students pay for permits, there must be a reliable way to connect the payment to the correct student and permit request. If confirmation depends on screenshots, paper receipts, or manual checking alone, errors can occur. A successful payment may not be recorded quickly, while an unverified payment may be accepted by mistake. Disputes can arise when students believe they have completed payment but the responsible officer cannot immediately confirm the transaction. Without a structured recovery process, these cases can remain unresolved for longer than necessary.

The problem extends beyond permits. Student governance includes communication, elections, document access, event announcements, and executive accountability. When these activities are managed through separate channels, students may not know which source contains the official information. An announcement may appear in one group but not another. Election information may be circulated informally. Documents may be shared through temporary links or personal devices. This makes communication inconsistent and reduces the SRC's ability to provide a dependable public record of its activities.

Election management can also suffer from manual limitations. Student elections require eligibility checks, candidate approval, controlled voting, and result management. If these processes are not properly structured, there may be questions about duplicate voting, ineligible voters, incomplete records, or unclear result visibility. Even where the election is conducted honestly, weak record management can create doubts because the process is difficult to verify afterward. In student governance, perceived fairness is almost as important as the final result because students need confidence in the process that produces their representatives.

Another problem is the lack of centralized auditability. Sensitive actions such as issuing permits, revoking permits, registering NFC cards, verifying payments, activating accounts, approving candidates, and publishing content should be traceable. In a manual system, the record of who performed an action may be incomplete or unavailable. This makes it harder to investigate mistakes, identify misuse, or explain decisions to stakeholders. A lack of audit logs also limits the ability of SRC executives and administrators to review operational patterns.

Poor operational visibility affects decision making. SRC executives may need to know how many permits have been issued, how many payments are pending, how many students have active permits, how many verification attempts failed, or which content has been published. If this information is stored across paper files, spreadsheets, receipts, and messaging channels, producing a simple report becomes difficult. The absence of a consolidated reporting view can lead to delayed decisions and weak oversight.

Mobile accessibility is another gap. Students increasingly rely on mobile devices for institutional communication and service access. If student governance services are available only through physical offices or desktop-centered systems, access becomes less convenient. A student may be unable to check permit status, follow election information, or submit a permit request without visiting an office or using a specific computer. Staff and security personnel may also need mobile tools for verification activities that occur away from an office desk.

The institutional problem can therefore be summarized as a lack of a coordinated, secure, and auditable student governance platform that connects permit management, payment verification, NFC-based identity checks, election voting, public communication, mobile access, and operational reporting. The existing manual and fragmented approach can lead to delays, errors, weak accountability, limited visibility, and reduced confidence in student governance workflows. This study addresses the problem by designing and implementing a centralized system for Knutsford University that supports these related activities within a controlled digital environment.

## 1.3 Aim of the Study

The aim of this study is to design and implement an NFC-based student permit verification and governance management system for Knutsford University that improves permit management, student verification, payment traceability, election voting, public communication, auditability, and mobile access within student governance operations.

## 1.4 Objectives of the Study

### General objective

The general objective of the study is to develop a centralized student governance platform that supports secure permit issuance, NFC-based verification, self-service permit requests, mobile access, election participation, public communication, audit logging, and operational reporting for Knutsford University.

### Specific objectives

The specific objectives of the study are to:

1. design a centralized operations dashboard for managing students, executives, roles, permissions, permits, payments, content, elections, and reports;
2. develop a permit management module that supports permit issuance, revocation, duplicate prevention, permit status tracking, and verification;
3. implement NFC-based verification to allow authorized users to verify student permit status using registered NFC cards;
4. provide alternative verification methods such as student number and permit code verification for cases where NFC is unavailable;
5. develop a self-service permit issuance workflow that allows eligible students to submit permit requests through a public or mobile channel;
6. integrate payment verification so that permit issuance can be linked to confirmed payment records;
7. create a mobile application access channel for student services, permit information, election voting, and authorized staff verification activities;
8. implement an election voting module that supports election setup, candidate approval, eligibility checks, voting, and result management;
9. provide a public portal for announcements, events, documents, executive information, election information, and self-service permit requests;
10. implement audit logging for sensitive actions such as permit issuance, payment verification, NFC card registration, account activation, and election voting;
11. develop reporting features that provide SRC executives and administrators with operational summaries for decision making;
12. enforce role-based access control so that users can only perform actions permitted by their assigned responsibilities;
13. evaluate the system through relevant tests covering authentication, permit workflows, payment verification, NFC card management, mobile API access, public content visibility, elections, and security restrictions.

## 1.5 Research Questions

The study is guided by the following research questions:

1. How can a centralized student governance platform improve permit management and reduce fragmentation in SRC operations?
2. How can NFC technology be applied to support faster and more reliable student permit verification?
3. How can a self-service permit issuance workflow improve student access to permit services while preserving administrative control?
4. How can payment verification be connected to permit issuance to reduce disputes and unverified permit generation?
5. How can a mobile application support student access, permit status viewing, election voting, and staff verification activities?
6. How can electronic voting be structured to support eligibility checks, candidate approval, and controlled student participation?
7. How can audit logging improve accountability in permit, payment, verification, account, and election workflows?
8. How can role-based access control protect administrative functions within a student governance platform?
9. How can public communication be improved through a structured portal for announcements, events, documents, and executive information?
10. How can operational reports assist SRC executives and university administrators in monitoring student governance activities?

## 1.6 Significance of the Study

This study is significant because it addresses practical problems in student governance using a technically grounded information system. Student permit verification, payment confirmation, elections, and public communication are not isolated administrative tasks. They affect how students experience governance and how executives account for their decisions. A system that improves these workflows can support both service delivery and institutional accountability.

For students, the proposed system can reduce the inconvenience associated with manual permit requests and verification. A student should not have to depend entirely on physical movement, informal updates, or repeated manual checks to know the status of a permit request. Self-service permit issuance and mobile access can make the process more accessible. Students can also benefit from more organized public communication through announcements, events, documents, and election information published through a public portal.

For SRC executives, the system provides a more structured way to manage governance activities. Executives can view operational information, monitor permit workflows, publish official content, manage elections, and review reports. This can reduce dependence on scattered spreadsheets, messaging groups, and paper records. It also gives executives a better basis for explaining decisions because sensitive actions can be connected to audit records.

For university administration, the study contributes to stronger oversight of student governance operations. A centralized system can help ensure that permits, payments, elections, and student records are handled through controlled workflows. Administrators can benefit from clearer records and reduced ambiguity when investigating disputes or reviewing SRC activities. The system also supports the broader institutional movement toward digital transformation by showing how student governance processes can be organized around reliable data.

Security personnel and authorized verification officers can also benefit from the system. Instead of relying only on visual inspection of permits, they can use NFC, permit code, or student number verification to check whether a student's permit is valid. Faster verification can reduce delays during operational activities, while logged verification events can provide evidence of checks performed.

Future researchers may use this study as a basis for further work in student identity management, NFC verification, mobile governance systems, digital payment integration, and electronic voting in educational institutions. The project combines several related concerns within one institutional platform, making it useful for examining the design tradeoffs involved in multi-module academic systems.

At the institutional level, the study supports digital transformation by demonstrating how student services can be made more coordinated and auditable. It does not assume that technology alone solves governance problems. Rather, it shows how carefully designed workflows, access control, verification, and reporting can support better administration when they are aligned with real institutional needs.

## 1.7 Scope of the Study

The scope of this study covers the design and implementation of a student governance platform for Knutsford University with emphasis on permit verification, student services, governance communication, mobile access, elections, auditability, and reporting.

The system includes an operations dashboard for authorized users such as administrators, SRC executives, and staff. The dashboard supports student management, executive management, role and permission management, academic periods, permit settings, permit issuance, payment oversight, NFC card management, verification logs, audit logs, reports, announcements, events, documents, polls, and elections. This dashboard is intended for controlled internal operations rather than general public use.

The study also includes a public portal. The public portal provides access to published announcements, events, documents, executive information, election information where appropriate, and self-service permit request pages. It is designed to separate public-facing content from internal administrative records.

The mobile application is included as a student and staff access channel. For students, it supports access to profile-related information, permit status, permit request workflows, and election voting where eligibility rules allow. For authorized staff or executives, it supports mobile operational workflows such as verification and selected permit-related activities. The mobile application depends on the mobile API for authenticated access to system functions.

Permit management is a central part of the scope. This includes creating permits, preventing duplicate active permits where applicable, revoking permits, tracking permit status, linking permits to students and academic periods, and supporting verification through permit-related identifiers. The scope also includes self-service permit issuance, where students can initiate permit requests and complete payment steps before permit processing is completed.

Payment integration is included for permit-related workflows. The system supports payment initialization, payment verification, and administrative recovery where a payment or permit request requires follow-up. The study focuses on connecting payment status to permit issuance decisions. It does not attempt to build a new payment gateway.

NFC verification is included as a major verification method. The scope covers registering NFC cards, linking cards to students, handling card lifecycle states such as replacement or revocation, and using NFC identifiers during verification. The system also supports non-NFC verification methods to reduce dependence on one hardware capability.

Election management is included to support student governance participation. The scope covers election setup, positions, candidates, approval workflows, eligibility checks, voting, and result management. The system is intended for student governance elections and related participation activities, not for national electoral processes or legally binding public elections.

The study includes content management for announcements, events, and documents. These modules support public communication and help the SRC publish information in a more organized way. Polling is also included as a participation feature, although it is treated separately from formal election voting because polls usually have lower procedural sensitivity.

Reporting and audit logging are within scope. Reports provide operational summaries for permits, payments, students, verifications, and other governance activities. Audit logs preserve records of sensitive actions and support accountability.

Some areas are outside the scope of this study. The system does not replace the university's full enterprise resource planning system, finance system, or official student information system. It does not provide biometric verification. It does not guarantee offline verification in all circumstances. It does not remove the need for administrative approval where institutional policy requires human review. It also does not attempt to solve all student governance challenges beyond the workflows implemented in the proposed platform.

## 1.8 Limitations of the Study

The study has some practical limitations that are common in software projects implemented within an academic period. These limitations do not remove the value of the system, but they define the conditions under which it is expected to operate.

The first limitation is dependence on internet connectivity. The dashboard, public portal, mobile API, payment verification, and most reporting functions require network access. If internet connectivity is poor, users may experience delays when submitting requests, verifying permits, or retrieving records. This limitation is important because student verification may sometimes occur in locations where connectivity is unstable.

The second limitation concerns NFC hardware availability. NFC-based verification requires a compatible NFC card and a device capable of reading NFC identifiers. Not all mobile devices support NFC, and some devices may support it differently depending on the operating system or hardware configuration. For this reason, the system includes alternative verification methods such as student number and permit code verification.

Platform-specific NFC behavior is also a limitation. Mobile operating systems may impose restrictions on how NFC can be read or written. Some devices may allow reading NFC cards but restrict background scanning or card writing. These constraints affect how the mobile application can support NFC workflows. The project therefore treats NFC as an important verification method while avoiding dependence on it as the only possible method.

Financial constraints also affect the study. A production deployment may require hosting infrastructure, domain configuration, SSL certificates, compatible NFC cards, mobile testing devices, and continued maintenance resources. The project can demonstrate the system design and implementation, but long-term institutional operation would require budget planning and administrative commitment.

Time constraints are another limitation. A final year project must be completed within a defined academic period. The system therefore focuses on the most relevant governance workflows rather than every possible extension. Features such as advanced analytics, offline NFC synchronization, biometric verification, and push notification campaigns can be considered for future work.

Deployment limitations may also apply. A complete production environment requires secure hosting, configured environment variables, queue workers, scheduled tasks, storage configuration, payment gateway keys, backups, and monitoring. If any of these deployment requirements are incomplete, the system may not operate at full production readiness. The study recognizes deployment as part of the project but does not assume unlimited infrastructure.

Operational assumptions also influence the system. The platform assumes that authorized users will be assigned appropriate roles, that student data entered into the system is accurate, that payment gateway credentials are properly configured, and that institutional rules for permit eligibility and election participation are clearly defined. A software system can enforce configured rules, but it cannot correct all policy ambiguities on its own.

## 1.10 Definition of Terms

Near Field Communication (NFC): NFC is a short-range wireless communication technology that allows compatible devices to read data from NFC cards or tags. In this study, NFC is used as one method for identifying a registered student card during permit verification.

Permit verification: Permit verification is the process of checking whether a student's permit is valid, active, and linked to the correct student and academic period. It may be performed using an NFC card, permit code, or student number.

Mobile application: A mobile application is software designed to run on mobile devices such as smartphones. In this study, the mobile application provides access to student services, permit information, election voting, and selected staff verification workflows.

Public portal: A public portal is the public-facing part of the system that provides access to published announcements, events, documents, executive information, election information, and self-service permit request pages.

Application Programming Interface (API): An API is a structured interface that allows one software component to communicate with another. In this study, the mobile API allows the mobile application to communicate with the backend system securely.

Audit logging: Audit logging is the recording of sensitive system actions for accountability and review. Examples include permit issuance, payment verification, account activation, NFC card registration, and election voting events.

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC): RBAC is an access control approach where system permissions are assigned according to user roles. In this study, RBAC helps ensure that students, executives, staff, and administrators can only perform actions appropriate to their responsibilities.

Self-service permit issuance: Self-service permit issuance is a workflow that allows students to initiate permit requests through a public or mobile channel, complete required payment steps, and have the request processed through the system.

Payment verification: Payment verification is the process of confirming that a payment transaction was successful before the related permit request is completed. It helps prevent permit issuance based on unverified payment evidence.

Sanctum authentication: Sanctum authentication refers to token-based authentication used to secure mobile API requests. In this study, it allows authenticated mobile users to access permitted student or staff operations.

Queue workers: Queue workers are background processes that handle tasks outside the immediate request cycle. They are useful for activities such as notifications, media processing, or other tasks that should not delay the user-facing request.

Electronic voting: Electronic voting is the use of a digital system to support vote casting, eligibility checks, candidate selection, and result handling. In this study, it refers to student governance election voting within the proposed platform.

Operations dashboard: The operations dashboard is the internal administrative interface used by authorized users to manage students, permits, payments, NFC cards, elections, content, reports, roles, and audit logs.

Student governance platform: A student governance platform is a digital system that supports administrative, communication, participation, and accountability workflows for student governance activities.
